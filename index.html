Why "Third Parent"

I became one before I had a word for it.

I supported my brother through his diagnosis with schizophrenia. No one handed me a manual. There was no school counselor trained to ask whether I was okay at home, no line on any intake form for "sibling of someone living with psychosis," no teacher who understood why a semester's grades slipped when a hospitalization did.

I became a third parent — not by training or by choice, but because someone had to hold things together. Not a parent. Not a professional. Something in between, doing work that rarely gets a name, let alone support.

Third Parent Initiative exists so the next caregiver doesn't have to figure it out alone, invisible, and unsupported — starting with the place where so many of us first go unseen: school.

"Caregiving for someone with a psychiatric illness doesn't come with a cast or a calendar of appointments people can see. It comes with silence, stigma, and a kid still expected to turn in homework on time."

— Founder, Third Parent Initiative

What We Offer

Resources for the person holding it together.

Every "third parent" needs different things at different moments. Here's what we provide, and what we're building toward.

Peer Support Groups

Facilitated spaces — in person and virtual — for people caring for a sibling, child, or parent living with serious mental illness. No fixing, no advice-giving required. Just people who understand what it's like to love someone through psychosis, mania, or a diagnosis that changes the shape of a family.

  • Weekly and drop-in group formats
  • Groups specifically for young & teen caregivers
  • Sibling-specific circles — a group often left out of family therapy

Caregiver Toolkits & Guides

Plain-language guides written for caregivers, not clinicians — because most of us learn about psychiatric illness in the middle of a crisis, not ahead of one.

  • Understanding schizophrenia, bipolar disorder & psychosis
  • Crisis & safety planning templates
  • "What to say / what not to say" conversation guides
  • A guide to talking to teachers and schools about caregiving

School Partnership Program

We work directly with schools to build policy that identifies student caregivers, and we train on-campus counselors to actually act on that information — extensions, excused absences, and real check-ins. This is the engine behind Our Plan.

  • Model intake-survey language for districts
  • Counselor training on accommodations for caregiving students
  • Ongoing outcome tracking with partner schools

Resource Navigation

One-on-one help connecting families to psychiatric care, financial and legal support, and respite — so caregivers aren't left to research all of this alone at 1am.

  • Referrals to psychiatric care and treatment programs
  • Guidance on guardianship, SSI/SSDI, and financial aid
  • Respite care connections for short-term relief

Our Theory of Change

You can't support a caregiver you can't see.

Schools already ask students dozens of intake questions. They almost never ask this one: "Do you help care for a family member living with a mental illness or disability?" Our plan starts there — and follows the thread all the way to the classroom.

1

Ask the question

We advocate for a simple caregiver-identification question inside existing school intake and wellness surveys — no new bureaucracy, just one honest question.

2

Reach out, directly

Students who identify as caregivers are contacted personally by our team or a trained school liaison — not left to find a pamphlet on their own.

3

Train the counselors

We work with on-campus counselors so they're equipped — and empowered — to grant deadline extensions, excused absences, and regular check-ins.

4

Connect to support

Every identified caregiver is offered a warm handoff into our support groups, toolkits, and resource navigation — not just a referral link.

5

Measure what matters

We track outcomes at every stage, so schools and funders can see the policy actually working — not just assume it does.

Research

The question I couldn't stop asking.

Before Third Parent Initiative was an organization, it was a question: how many students are already doing this — quietly, unpaid, and completely unmeasured?

0%

13.8% of middle and high school students report missing school to care for someone else — a family member managing illness, disability, or age. In some districts, that number climbs past 35%.

Why this number matters more than it looks

13.8% isn't a rounding error. In an average school of 1,000 students, that's roughly 138 kids managing a responsibility no attendance policy, grading rubric, or college application accounts for. And that number is almost certainly an undercount — because "caring for someone" on a survey usually gets read as grandma's broken hip, not a sibling in a psychiatric hospital.

Why psychiatric caregiving hides even deeper

A student who misses school for a parent's chemotherapy can usually say so out loud. A student who misses school because their brother is in a psychiatric unit, or because someone at home is in crisis, usually can't — not without inviting stigma, disbelief, or a very complicated conversation with a teacher who's never been trained to hold it. Physical caregiving is visible. Psychiatric caregiving is disclosed only when it can't be hidden anymore. That gap is exactly what Third Parent Initiative is built to close.

What we're building from here

Our theory of change (see Our Plan) is a direct response to this research: if we don't ask the identifying question, we can't measure the gap — and if we can't measure it, schools have no reason to act on it. We're partnering with schools to run the caregiver-identification question, then tracking grades before and after accommodations are put in place.

Monthly Newsletter

The Third Parent Letter

A monthly note on caregiving, psychiatry, and the things I wish someone had told me — opinions and all.

One email a month. Unsubscribe anytime.

A broken leg gets a cast, a card, a casserole from a neighbor. A psychiatric diagnosis in the family gets silence. As caregivers, we're taught — by stigma, by law, by well-meaning privacy protections — to say as little as possible about what's happening at home. HIPAA protects the patient, and it should. But it also means the person doing the driving to appointments, the de-escalating, the up-all-night watching, often has no legal standing to even be told the diagnosis, let alone supported for carrying its weight.

Physical illness is legible to institutions. Psychiatric illness is not — and neither, by extension, is the person caring for someone living with it. Schools have started to build language around caregiving for a grandparent with dementia or a sibling with a physical disability. Almost none have language for a student going home to a parent mid-episode or a sibling in crisis. Until we name it, we can't policy our way toward supporting it. That's the whole premise of this organization.

People ask why Third Parent Initiative focuses on caregivers of people with serious psychiatric illness — schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, psychosis — instead of the broader, more fundable, more comfortable banner of "mental health." Here's the honest answer: mental health has a marketing budget. Psychiatric illness still doesn't.

"Mental health" today conjures therapy apps, mindfulness, stress management — important, but a different category of experience than caring for someone through a psychotic episode, an involuntary hold, or a lifetime diagnosis that reshapes a household. Caregivers in that world face a sharper stigma, higher stakes, and far fewer resources built specifically for them. I chose "psychiatric" because I don't want the specificity of what my family went through to get diluted into a wellness hashtag. The caregivers I'm trying to reach need something built for exactly their situation — not adjacent to it.
